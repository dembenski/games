Remembrance Day · 11 November 2026

The Eatmans
who served

From Elm Hill and Fredericton, New Brunswick, the sources name at least eight men from this Black family who served in the two world wars — a father and son in two different wars, a world-class sprinter who had to cross an ocean to enlist, and two brothers in Canada's Black battalion. One did not come home. The evidence is not equal for all eight: five rest on official, archival or index records, Eldridge's on secondary sources (his file was destroyed), Dubert's on two sources, and one brother on a single museum source — each entry below is flagged with its strength.

A note on the name: military records consistently spell the family Eatman — Library and Archives Canada, the Canadian Virtual War Memorial, and the No. 2 Construction Battalion nominal roll all use it. Eatmon is the family variant: Chester Eatmon “later changed his last name to Eatmon after marrying Edna” (MyNewBrunswick), and Ancestry indexes one soldier as “Manzer Eatmon [Manzer Eatman].” Eldridge also appears as Eastman. Anyone searching the archives should try all three spellings. Researched 11 November 2026; every claim below carries its source.

Sergeant Arthur Wallace “Duke” Eatman

Arthur Wallace Eatman 1920–1943 · age 23

Killed in action — Sicily

Carleton and York Regiment, R.C.I.C. · Service number G/18010

Born
15 May 1920, Fredericton, NB
Parents
James W. & Thursa L. Eatman, Barker's Point, York Co.
Enlisted
September 1939, Woodstock, NB — see date note below
Died
22 July 1943, Sicily
Buried
Agira Canadian War Cemetery, grave C, C, 288
Commemorated
Page 156, Second World War Book of Remembrance

Arthur Eatman enlisted in Woodstock in September 1939, aged 19, and rose steadily: Private, then Corporal in 1940, then Sergeant by 1942 (New Brunswick Museum). The Carleton and York Regiment went overseas with the 1st Canadian Division in December 1939, and in 1943 took part in the Allied invasion of Sicily — Operation Husky (UNB Art Centre; NB Museum).

On 22 July 1943, during a reconnaissance patrol, twenty-eight men from A Company under Eatman's command were tasked with checking a nearby residence for enemy forces. The patrol did not return. The following day, five bodies — including Eatman's — were recovered; another soldier's remains were never found. The remaining twenty-two men were taken prisoner (New Brunswick Museum).

Two honest hedges. ① The Museum's account does not name where the patrol took place, and a CommunityStories page describes him as “killed in action at Agira” — but Agira is confirmed as his burial place, not necessarily the place of death. ② His enlistment date is disputed: the Canadian Virtual War Memorial gives 7 September 1939; the NB Museum gives 3 September 1939. The discrepancy is unresolved — possibly attestation vs. attestation-paper date — and only his service file can settle it.
“Amongst the only visible examples of a Black non-commissioned officer serving in a non-segregated unit.” — UNB Art Centre, Black History Month project, on Arthur “Duke” Eatman

A 2012 York-Sunbury Historical Society talk by Dr. Lee Windsor was titled “A Black Leader of White Men: Sergeant Arthur Eatman with the Carleton and York Regiment at War in Sicily, 1943.” His name — “Arthur W. Eatman” — is inscribed on the Carleton and York Regiment Memorial in Fredericton, dedicated 8 May 1998 (Veterans Affairs Canada). A UNB study, “Making it up as you go: racial policy in the Canadian military during the two world wars,” uses the personnel files of James and Arthur Eatman to examine wartime policy toward Black soldiers.

Awards: no individual gallantry award or honour is recorded for him — the CWGC honours field is blank, and no source consulted lists campaign medals. His WWII service file exists in Library and Archives Canada's WWII Records and Service Files of War Dead, 1939–1947 (index: G18010, Volume range 25713–25812); the full digitised file was not opened in this research. The CVWM page carries a portrait of him in uniform and a digital gallery with a newspaper clipping, the Fredericton War Memorial, and Agira cemetery images.

Sources: Canadian Virtual War Memorial · New Brunswick Museum, “A Snapshot in Time” (2 Feb 2026) · UNB Art Centre · VAC — Carleton and York Regiment Memorial · UNB Scholar study

His father — Pte James Wallace Eatman 1882–1956

Son of
Major & Hannah (Holmes) Eatman

A generation before his son fell in Sicily, James Wallace Eatman served in France with the 25th Battalion. The New Brunswick Museum records that he participated in the capture of Vimy Ridge in 1917 and was wounded at the Battle of Passchendaele. His wife is listed in the service index as Therasa / Thursa Levina Wright.

Source strength: the Vimy and Passchendaele details rest on the NB Museum article and the UNB thesis that used his personnel file — his LAC file itself was not read in this research, and no LAC box reference was retrieved. Family details such as his parents' names come from a genealogy forum post, a weaker source, and are flagged as such here.

Sources: New Brunswick Museum (2026) · Forces War Records index of the LAC WWI collection · Genealogy.com forum (weaker source)

First World War

The sprinter who crossed an ocean to enlist

Eldridge “Gus” Eatman 1880–1960

Before he was a soldier, Gus Eatman was a world-class sprinter — the Canadian Encyclopedia calls him exactly that. When war broke out he was in New Brunswick, but the Canadian Army was not accepting Black Canadian recruits, so he went to England and enlisted in the British Army in 1915. A photograph of him in uniform doing rifle drill appeared on the front page of the British Daily Mirror on 6 March 1915. He was promoted to corporal, wounded in the leg at Loos, and — per the secondary accounts — his battalion transferred to Salonika in late October 1915, remaining until June 1918. The NB Black History Society credits him with 785 days in frontline trenches. He was inducted into the New Brunswick Sports Hall of Fame in 2016, in part through the efforts of his third cousin, Maurice Eatman (Athletics Canada).

Read this with care: Eatman's British service record was destroyed in a fire during the London Blitz in the Second World War (Canadian Encyclopedia). That means the 785-days figure, the exact battalion, and the Loos / Salonika timeline rest on secondary sources — the Encyclopedia, CommunityStories, the NB Black History Society, the Sports Hall of Fame, and Athletics Canada — and have not been verified against a service record. They are presented here as those sources report them, not as archival fact. The Daily Mirror photograph is a contemporary corroboration, though it was not itself viewed in this research.

Not service: in 1935, as “Eastman,” he campaigned to raise an Ethiopian Foreign Legion against Mussolini's invasion of Ethiopia. No Canadian legion went to Ethiopia — it is part of his story, but it is not military service and is not counted as such here.

Sources: The Canadian Encyclopedia · CommunityStories — Our Black Heritage · NB Black History Society · NB Sports Hall of Fame · Athletics Canada (2023)

The Black Battalion

No. 2 Construction Battalion

Created 5 July 1916, No. 2 Construction Battalion was a segregated, non-combatant unit — the first and only all-Black battalion-sized formation in Canadian military history. Formed in Pictou, Nova Scotia, it boarded SS Southland on 28 March 1917, arrived in Liverpool on 7 April 1917, and served in the Jura region of eastern France from 17 May 1917 with the Canadian Forestry Corps — cutting timber, building and maintaining roads, railways, and camp services. It was disbanded in September 1920. A National Apology was issued on 9 July 2022; the battle honour “France and Flanders, 1917–18” was awarded 1 June 2022, and the battalion is perpetuated by 4 Engineer Support Regiment, CFB Gagetown, NB (Canada.ca). Two Eatmans were on its strength.

Pte John Wesley Eatman

Regimental number 931125 · b. 18 April 1898

On the battalion's nominal roll of 28 March 1917: next of kin William Bateman Eatman, Fredericton, N.B.; enlisted at Pictou, 14 August 1916. The Fredericton Region Museum lists him as “son of William B. Eatman of Zealand.” His LAC record: CEF, RG 150, Accession 1992-93/166, Box 2812-14.

Sources: Nova Scotia Archives nominal roll · Library and Archives Canada · Fredericton Region Museum (2022)

Pte Manzer Eatman

Regimental number 931124 · b. 6 April 1882 · consecutive to John's

Also on the 28 March 1917 nominal roll: next of kin William Eatman, Washington, D.C., U.S.A.; enlisted at Pictou, 14 August 1916 — the same day as John, with the next regimental number. The Fredericton Region Museum lists him as “brother of Eldridge and William B. Eatman of Zealand.” Ancestry's CEF attestation index lists him as “Manzer Eatmon [Manzer Eatman].”

Sources: Nova Scotia Archives nominal roll · National Apology Advisory Committee report · Fredericton Region Museum (2022)

A relationship label that doesn't quite line up: the CommunityStories Tyler page calls John “Eldridge Eatman's cousin,” while the Museum calls Manzer Eldridge's brother and John the son of William B. Eatman. If William B. is Eldridge's brother, John would be Eldridge's nephew, not his cousin. This page does not assert either label without a family-tree source. His attestation paper was read in full at Library and Archives Canada (Item 371828, RG 150, Box 2812-17): born 6 April 1882 in Fredericton; 5 ft 9 in, chest 40 in, Black complexion, eyes and hair, Methodist, married, trade “Farmer” (the handwriting is uncertain). One conflict remains: the paper’s next of kin reads like his wife — the name looks like “Eldridge Eatman” — while the nominal roll names William Eatman of Washington, D.C. Neither is settled without a re-read of the image. All of the York and Sunbury County men travelled overseas with the battalion in March 1917 (Fredericton Region Museum).

Mansfield Eatmon · b. 22 January 1888, Elm Hill

Mansfield Eatmon is in the same CEF attestation index, but no regimental number, unit, box number or enlistment detail was found in any record consulted. Checked & cleared up

Chester Eatmon was not a veteran

Chester Adolphus Eatmon (1904–1988) — the harness racer

Born in Elm Hill on 7 June 1904 to William Bateman Eatman and Rosella Blanch McIntyre, Chester married Edna McCarty on 10 November 1925 — and, per MyNewBrunswick, changed his surname to Eatmon after marrying her. He became a fixture at Fredericton Raceway for 26 years and, at 82, was Canada's oldest licensed harness racing driver. The UNB Art Centre lists him as “Harness racer,” not a soldier — and no record of military service was found for him in any source consulted. He died in Fredericton in July 1988 (MyNewBrunswick says 21 July; the WikiTree Sunny Bank Cemetery listing says 25 July — a minor, unresolved discrepancy) and is buried in Sunny Bank Cemetery, Devon.

Don't confuse him with a different Chester Eatmon (b. 21 December 1947, U.S. Army, Vietnam era, d. 21 October 1973) who appears in the U.S. VA BIRLS death file. That is a different man.

Sources: MyNewBrunswick — Chester Eatmon · UNB Art Centre

Wider circle

Their neighbours in uniform

Not Eatmans — but men from the same Fredericton and York–Sunbury Black community whose service frames the family's. Included as context, clearly labelled as not kin unless a source says so.

Bugle Sgt Arthur “Seymour” Tyler (1897–1985)

Enlisted in No. 2 Construction Battalion on 14 September 1916 — one of ten Fredericton-region men in the battalion — and later served as a WWII Bugle Sergeant with the Carleton and York Regiment. His leg was broken in seven places in bombing during the Battle of Britain in 1941; he returned to Canada that year and died 17 February 1985, age 88.

Correction on record: the NB Black History Society repeats a story that Tyler served with the “Canadian Black Devils from Winnipeg” / 8th Battalion. The Black Canadian Veterans site states explicitly that this is false — “Seymour was never a member of the 8th Canadian Infantry Battalion (90th Rifles).”

Sources: CommunityStories · Black Canadian Veterans

Sapper Miles Smith Dymond (b. 1882)

Of Fredericton; 1st (Brighton) Field Company, Canadian Engineers. Enlisted at Valcartier 23 September 1914, in France 18 March 1915: 2nd Ypres, the Somme, Vimy Ridge, Hill 70, Passchendaele — buried by shell fire on at least three occasions. Discharged December 1918. His daughter Thea married No. 2 veteran Reuben Johnson.

Dymond is also Eldridge Eatman's mother's maiden name (Jane Diamond / Dymond) — but no source consulted proves Miles's exact kinship to Eldridge, so none is claimed here.

Sources: Roberts Publications · VAC — Black Canadians in uniform

Honest gaps

What was not found

  • Korea and later wars: searches found no Eatman / Eatmon Korea veteran and no other Eatman / Eatmon war-dead entry — Arthur is the only Eatman in the Canadian Virtual War Memorial. That is an absence in the searches consulted, not proof none served; the CVWM surname search did not function as a filter when tested, and LAC post-1945 files are ATIP-only — it was not checked against LAC post-war personnel records or the Korean War Book of Remembrance.
  • Elm Hill's intermarried families: intermarriage is documented — Prudence Blanche Radcliffe (b. Nov 1899, Elm Hill, to Ernest Radcliffe and Elizabeth Eatmon) married Marshall Bowles on 24 October 1918 at Amherst, NS, then Thomas Parris on 26 December 1922 (WikiTree, citing census records). But no military service was found for Marshall Bowles, Ernest Radcliffe, or James Henry Bowles (1888–1957) — father of wrestler Rocky Johnson and grandfather of Dwayne Johnson. A 2026 genealogical post says the McIntyre, Bowles, Radcliffe, and Eatmon families of Elm Hill “frequently intermarried” — a social-media genealogy claim, not an archival source.
  • The one McIntyre in No. 2: the nominal roll lists 931102, Pte Charles Theodore McIntyre (next of kin Mrs. Hannah McIntyre, 139 Mecklenburg St., Saint John; enlisted Saint John, 10 August 1916). He is from Saint John, not Elm Hill, and no source connects him to Rosella Blanch McIntyre, Chester's mother, or to the Elm Hill McIntyres. He is not claimed as family. (Nova Scotia Archives)
  • Maurice Eatman — Eldridge's third cousin who campaigned for his Sports Hall of Fame induction — has no military service found.
Next steps

Open questions

  1. Arthur's enlistment date — 3 Sept 1939 (NB Museum) vs 7 Sept 1939 (CVWM). His LAC war-dead service file (Vol. 25713–25812) should settle it.
  2. Arthur's campaign medals — none recorded in CWGC / CVWM; his service file should list them.
  3. Where Arthur died — the patrol's location is unnamed by the Museum; Agira is confirmed as burial only.
  4. Dubert Eatman — his service is now corroborated by the 2019 UNB thesis, but no obituary or separate service record was found. Harry Ellis Eatman — still one source only; needs U.S. NARA follow-up.
  5. Manzer Eatman — date of birth (6 April 1882) and LAC box (2812-17) now known from his attestation paper; the next-of-kin conflict and his post-war life are still untraced.
  6. Joseph Eatmon — unit, LAC box number, enlistment date and place, occupation and physical description not obtained; his attestation image was not read.
  7. James Wallace Eatman — LAC box reference not retrieved; Vimy / Passchendaele rest on the Museum article and UNB thesis, not a file read in this research.
  8. Eldridge Eatman — British file destroyed in the Blitz; the 785 days, exact battalion, and Loos / Salonika details remain secondary-source only.
  9. Korea / post-1945 — no Eatman/Eatmon found, but the CVWM surname search did not work as a filter when tested, and LAC post-1945 files are ATIP-only; the negative is not conclusive.
  10. Bowles / Radcliffe / McIntyre service — none found for the Elm Hill families.
  11. Chester's death date — 21 July 1988 (MyNewBrunswick) vs 25 July 1988 (WikiTree cemetery listing). Minor and unresolved.
